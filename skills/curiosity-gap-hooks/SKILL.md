# Curiosity Gap Hooks

**Source:** The Rest Is Science podcast, episode on curiosity (Hannah Fry and Michael
Mosley), https://youtu.be/YRk7xGpD8dI. Research base: information-gap theory (Loewenstein
1994), the wrong-guess retention study (1992), the "Pandora" shock-pen study (2016), the
curiosity-dampening study (2020). When any of this is cited inside output copy, cite it as
"a 1994 Carnegie Mellon paper" or "a 2016 study", never by researcher name. A year and an
institution read more human than a surname.

**The one sentence:** a hook is not a promise that the content is good. A hook is a hole
the reader can point at, that you refuse to fill for a moment, that the body then fills
exactly.

**Related skills, so you pick the right one:**
- Story-ingredient hooks (Person, Location, Action, Cost, Era) are a different craft. Use a
  story-hook skill for "tell the story" openers. Combine them: story details supply the
  concrete, this skill supplies the hole.
- Whole-video retention (progressive disclosure, loop reopening, visual blanks) belongs to
  a video-script skill. Use it when the deliverable is a full script.
- This skill covers the opening itself, in any format, built from the science below.

## 1. The science, compressed (read once, apply always)

**Curiosity is a deprivation, not a wish.** It is the distance between what the reader
knows and what they have *just realised* they don't know. The "just realised" is the whole
trick: you manufacture the realisation. Analogy from the episode: a missing tooth. The
tongue will not leave it alone because it knows exactly where the tooth should be. A vague
"I don't know much about 16th-century French poetry" is not a gap. "I know six of the seven
dwarfs" is a gap.

**The Goldilocks curve.** Zero knowledge = no itch. Complete knowledge = no itch. The peak
is in the middle: the reader knows enough to feel the exact shape of the hole and believes
they can fill it. Babies shown a ball passing through a wall bash that ball against every
surface. Too elaborate a trick, they ignore it. Too simple, they ignore it. Your reader's
existing knowledge decides where the peak is. A gap that works on agency owners is noise
to a plumber.

**The Pandora effect.** Told exactly which pens deliver a shock, people touch none. Told
only "some of these will shock you", they touch them five times as often. Ambiguity with a
known shape beats a fact. "One of these three things is killing your page" beats "your
headline is killing your page".

**Wanting is not liking.** Curiosity runs on the dopamine wanting system, the same one
that fires at a fruit machine or a text back from someone you fancy. It motivates. It
does not satisfy. The hook borrows the wanting system. The body must deliver the liking.
Trigger wanting without delivering liking and you have built doomscroll junk.

**Primed memory.** A reader in the curious state retains the answer far better. "Your
stomach lining regenerates every three days" is forgettable. "Your stomach acid can melt
metal, so why aren't you melting from the inside out?" then guesses, then the reveal,
and the fact sticks. Open the gap BEFORE the lesson and the lesson lands.

**Wrong guesses are fuel.** Asked which US state reaches furthest east, most say Maine.
Told "wrong" (it is Alaska, it crosses the date line), people become more curious AND
remember the answer longer than people who guessed and moved on. Invite the guess. Tell
them the obvious answer is wrong. Then hold.

**Information is its own reward.** Given two levers with identical odds of food, humans,
monkeys and birds press the one that reveals the outcome early. Goldfish don't care.
Your readers will pay attention for information alone. You do not need to bribe them
with hype, you need to make the information feel withheld and obtainable.

**Morbid curiosity is rehearsal.** Guppies that swim out to inspect a predator get eaten
less. People high in morbid curiosity coped better in early 2020. "What went wrong" stories
pull because the reader is rehearsing danger safely. Failure post-mortems are premium gap
material.

**Snacking kills hunger.** Feeds that resolve, resolve, resolve every 30 seconds never let
a gap stay open long enough to matter, and over time they dampen curiosity itself. Two
lessons: (1) do not resolve every line, let the hold do its work; (2) do not publish
gap-only content that never delivers a meal. Readers who are hungry for real information
need to be fed.

**Specific beats vague.** "You'll enjoy this" opens nothing. "Number one is mind-blowing"
is literally "what's in the envelope?" The gap must have a shape the reader can name.

**The hook-and-tease template** (the envelope from the episode): a container with the
reader's name on it, its contents described but not shown, an explicit promise that they
will want it more as the piece progresses, a refusal to open it when asked, and a return
to it later. Every device in this skill is a variation of that envelope.

## 2. The protocol: OPEN, HOLD, GUESS, PAY

1. **OPEN.** Create a gap where the reader nearly knows. Give five dwarfs, ask for two.
   Name the container. Name the missing piece's shape (a word, a number, a cause, a step).
2. **HOLD.** Refuse to close it immediately. No instant gratification. The hold is made of
   evidence that helps them solve it, never filler. Length of hold scales with format
   (see table).
3. **GUESS.** Invite a guess, or pre-empt the obvious wrong one ("most people say price;
   it isn't price"). Being told the obvious answer is wrong is the single strongest
   retention lever in the research.
4. **PAY.** Close the gap with the specific thing you named. Literally true. The same
   shape you promised. A "one word" gap pays with one word, not a paragraph of context.

| Format | Where the gap opens | How long the hold runs | Where it pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn post | line 1 (before "see more") | lines 2 to ~8 | inside the post, never "link in comments" for the core answer |
| Email | subject + first line | 2 to 4 short paragraphs | inside the email, or the click IS the named payoff |
| Article intro | first two sentences | first section | H2 that names the answer, then the rest |
| Video cold open | first 3 seconds | until at least 40% of runtime, with one reopen | on screen, shown not just said |
| Ad headline | headline | body copy (1 to 2 lines) | landing page H1 must pay the same gap |
| Landing page H1 | H1 | hero + first section | above the fold or first scroll |
| Outreach first line | sentence 1 | sentence 2 | the reply offer ("want the second one?") |

## 3. Gap shapes (pick ONE per opening)

| Shape | Template | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Nearly-complete list** | "[N minus 1] of the [N] are obvious. The last one is the one that [cost/decided it]." | "Four questions every buyer asks before paying. Most pages answer two. The two they skip are the same two, every time." |
| **Contradiction** | "[Established fact]. So why [thing that should not follow]?" | "A bakery's landing page scored 28 out of 100. Sales were fine. Six months later the same page scored 84 and sales doubled. What changed was not the photos." |
| **Wrong-guess bait** | "[Question]. Most people say [obvious answer]. It isn't." | "400 comments, 9 buyers. Ask any marketer why and they say the CTA. It wasn't the CTA." |
| **Sealed container** | "I wrote [X] down before [event]. [Reader] will want it more as this goes on." | "I wrote the reason on a card before the call started. The client asked me to turn it over three times. I turned it over at minute 40." |
| **Pre-announced hole** | "At some point in the next [N] [lines/minutes] I'm going to show you [X]. You'll know the moment." | "Somewhere in the next six lines there is a number you will not be able to leave alone." |
| **Named-number gap** | "There are [N]. Number [K] is the one [that matters]." | "Three reasons a cold email gets ignored. The third one is the one that cost a founder a year of pipeline." |
| **Missing word** | "One word in [thing] [caused outcome]. It's not the word you think." | "One word in a coach's headline drops its clarity score from 8 to 2. It's a word every coach uses." |
| **Consequence gap** | "[Person] did [small action]. [Timeframe] later, [big outcome]. Here's what connected them." | "A founder sent 67 LinkedIn replies. 21 days later: a £625K pipeline. They did not post once. So what were they doing all day?" |

Rules for the table: every number must be true and sourced from the writer's real records.
Every named person is a first name. Every gap in the table has a payoff the writer can
actually deliver. The examples above are illustrative, so swap in your own real numbers.

## 4. DOS

- **Do find the payoff first.** Write the closing line before the opening line. If you
  cannot write the payoff in one specific sentence, you do not have a gap, you have a topic.
- **Do give the gap a shape.** A word, a number, a cause, a step, a name, a reason. The
  reader must be able to say what is missing.
- **Do calibrate to the reader's knowledge.** Open on ground they already stand on (their
  own page, their own buyers, their own inbox), then remove one brick.
- **Do use a container when you can.** A card, a screenshot face down, a number written
  before the call, an envelope. Physical containers make the gap visible.
- **Do invite a guess** or pre-empt the obvious wrong one. "Guess before you scroll."
  "Most people say price. It isn't price."
- **Do hold with evidence.** Every line of the hold should make the reader more able to
  solve it, not just more impatient.
- **Do return to the gap explicitly.** "Remember that number from line one." A callback
  is the cheapest retention device there is.
- **Do pay in the promised shape.** One-word gap pays with one word. "The reason" pays with
  a reason, in one sentence, then the explanation.
- **Do keep the headline literally true.** If the payoff is "it depends", the hook must not
  promise "the one reason".
- **Do use failure and near-misses.** Morbid curiosity is rehearsal. "The week I lost the
  €500K blog" outpulls "how I built a €500K blog".
- **Do use one gap per short piece.** LinkedIn post: one. Email: one. A video can carry a
  primary gap plus one reopen.
- **Do run the humanizer** on the finished hook + body before delivery, then the
  unnatural-phrasing pass (real reader's ear, then the client's ear).

## 5. DON'TS

- **Don't close the gap in the hook.** "Here's why 400 comments made 9 sales: the CTA was
  weak" is a summary, not a hook.
- **Don't open a gap you don't pay.** That is clickbait. It also trains the reader that
  the hooks are junk, and junk snacks dampen hunger over time. One unpaid gap costs the
  next ten hooks.
- **Don't use promise-words instead of a shape.** Banned as gap substitutes: "you won't
  believe", "game-changer", "this changes everything", "must read", "you'll love this",
  "read till the end", "mind-blowing" (unless it names WHICH item), "nobody is talking
  about this".
- **Don't ask empty rhetorical questions.** "Ever wondered why your ads don't convert?" has
  no missing tooth. "Your ads convert at 1.2%. The same audience converted at 0.9% with one
  sentence, then 3.1% after one change to the second sentence. Which sentence?" does.
- **Don't open gaps on ground the reader doesn't stand on.** Zero knowledge = zero itch.
  A gap about entity SEO is dead to a florist. Translate it into their world first.
- **Don't stack mysteries.** Three open questions in one opener is the elaborate magic
  trick the babies ignore. One hole, clearly shaped.
- **Don't make it guessable in one second.** No deprivation, no drive. If the obvious
  guess is right, the hook is dead. Make the obvious guess wrong.
- **Don't pad the hold.** Filler between hook and payoff reads as stalling and the reader
  leaves. The hold is built from evidence, not from "but first".
- **Don't over-hold for the format.** A LinkedIn post that pays in the comments, an email
  that pays "next week", a video that pays in the last 5 seconds after 10 minutes of
  stalling: all of these break trust. Hold long enough to itch, never long enough to bore.
- **Don't invent or round.** Numbers, names, outcomes, timelines: literally true only.
  The gap is only as strong as the reader's trust that the payoff is real.
- **Don't name the mechanism.** Never "here's a curiosity gap for you". The envelope works
  because it is an envelope, not because it is explained.
- **Don't shrug at the payoff.** If the answer to the wrong-guess bait is "well, it
  depends", cut the bait.
- **Don't use banned words, last names, or em dashes in public copy** if your house style
  forbids them. Keep your own banned-word list. LinkedIn: one sentence per line, blank
  line between.

## 6. Good vs bad, per format

Every BAD line names the failed test. Every GOOD line names the gap shape and includes
the payoff so the hold is provable.

### LinkedIn opener

BAD:
> 5 content lessons that will change your business 🚀 Read till the end!

Fails the tooth test (what is missing? "lessons" has no shape), the specific-shape test
(promise-word "change your business"), and it resolves nothing because nothing was opened.

GOOD (wrong-guess bait):
> 400 people commented on one post.
>
> 9 of them bought.
>
> Every marketer I showed it to blamed the same thing.
>
> They were all wrong.

Hold: lines 5 to 8 show what the 400 commenters had in common (peers, not buyers) and
what the 9 had in common. Guess pre-empted ("the CTA"). Payoff: "The post was written in
creator language. The 9 buyers were the only ones who recognised their own problem in it."
One sentence, same shape as the gap ("the same thing").

### Email subject + first line

BAD:
> Subject: Our latest insights on buyer psychology
> hey {{ subscriber.first_name }}, hope you're well! This week I wanted to share some thoughts on...

Fails Goldilocks (no ground the reader stands on), tooth (nothing missing), hold (there is
nothing to hold).

GOOD (contradiction):
> Subject: 28 to 84, same page
> hey {{ subscriber.first_name }},
>
> A bakery's landing page scored 28 out of 100 on the buyer psychology check.
>
> Sales were fine. The owner nearly didn't change it.
>
> Six months later the same page scored 84 and sales doubled.
>
> Not the headline. Not the price. Guess before you scroll.

Hold: two short paragraphs of what the check measures. Payoff: the one section the owner
rewrote, in buyers' own words from their reviews. Click is optional; the answer is in
the email.

### Article intro

BAD:
> In this article we will explore why buyer language matters for AI search and how
> businesses can adapt their content strategy accordingly.

Fails everything. It is a table of contents in prose.

GOOD (consequence gap):
> A founder sent 67 LinkedIn replies in 21 days.
>
> At the end of the 21 days the pipeline showed £625K.
>
> They did not publish a single post in that time.
>
> So what were they typing all day?

Hold: the first section describes what a reply is worth versus a post, with the numbers.
Payoff H2: "They were answering buyers' questions in their own words, in their comments,
not their own." The rest of the article is the method.

### Video cold open (first 3 seconds)

BAD:
> Hey guys, today I'm going to talk about why your landing page isn't converting.

Fails tooth, specific-shape and hold. "Why it isn't converting" is a topic, not a hole.

GOOD (sealed container + pre-announced hole):
> [card held face down] I wrote the reason your page isn't converting on this before you
> clicked. It's one of three things. By the end you'll know which one is yours. Most of
> you will guess the wrong one.

Hold: show the three, with a real example for each. Reopen at 50%: "Still think it's the
headline?" Payoff: turn the card over on screen. The card must say something specific
enough to be wrong.

### Ad headline

BAD:
> Grow your business with data-driven marketing

Fails tooth, Goldilocks, Pandora (nothing ambiguous, nothing to act on).

GOOD (missing word):
> Your buyers describe their problem in words that are not on your website.
> The report shows you which ones.

Pandora effect: they know their website, they know their buyers, they do not know which
words. Landing page H1 must pay the same gap (show a real example of the mismatch above
the fold). Only run it where the report can literally deliver the words.

### Landing page H1

BAD:
> The #1 AI Copy Diagnostic Tool

Fails tooth. Superlatives are promise-words.

GOOD (nearly-complete list):
> Every buyer asks four questions before they pay.
> Find out which two your page skips.

Hold: the four questions listed in the hero, unanswered for the visitor's page. Payoff:
the scan result. "Which two" is a claim; if the tool sometimes finds one or three,
write "which ones" instead. Literally true or not at all.

### Outreach first line (DM / cold email)

BAD:
> Hi Sam, I love what you're doing! I help coaches scale with AI-powered marketing
> systems and would love to...

Fails tooth, and it is about the sender, not about Sam's ground.

GOOD (contradiction + guess invite):
> Sam, I ran your page through my diagnostic.
>
> Persuasion scored 4.3 out of 10. Clarity scored 2.
>
> The distance between those two numbers is one sentence. Want to see which?

Hold: the reply. Payoff: the sentence, with the fix, in the next message, no pitch
attached. State the score, do not diagnose the person's character.

## 7. The 7 gap tests (score every variant before showing the writer)

| # | Test | Question | Pass |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Tooth** | Can the reader say in five words what is missing? | Yes, in one shape (word / number / cause / step / name) |
| 2 | **Goldilocks** | Does the target reader already stand on the ground the gap is cut into? | Yes, uses their page / buyers / inbox / numbers |
| 3 | **Pandora** | Is there ambiguity they would act to resolve, not a fact they'd nod at? | "One of three", "which one", "not the one you think" |
| 4 | **Specific shape** | Would it survive replacing "this" with the actual thing? No promise-words? | No banned promise-words; the shape is named |
| 5 | **Hold** | Is the gap still open after the first line? After the third? | Yes, and the hold is evidence, not filler |
| 6 | **Guess** | Can the reader form a wrong guess, or is the obvious guess pre-empted? | Yes |
| 7 | **Payoff** | Does the body literally deliver the named shape, truthfully? | Yes, same shape, one sentence, verifiable |

Pass rule: 7 of 7 for ads, landing pages and outreach (money on the line). 6 of 7 for
LinkedIn and email, but test 7 (payoff) can never be the one that fails.

Display per variant, one line: `Gap: Tooth ✓ Goldilocks ✓ Pandora ✓ Shape ✓ Hold ✓ Guess ✓ Payoff ✓ · shape: wrong-guess bait`

## 8. Workflow

1. **Input.** The writer gives a topic, story, receipt, draft, or a flat opener. Identify the
   format (table in section 2) and the target reader.
2. **Payoff first.** Write the one-sentence answer the piece delivers. If the writer has not
   given it, ask for it or pull it from their own real results.
3. **Check the ground.** What does this reader already know that the gap can be cut into?
   If nothing, translate the payoff into their world before opening any gap.
4. **Pick one gap shape per variant** from section 3. Variant count = what the writer asks for ("10 versions" = 10), default 5. Use a different shape for each until all 8 shapes are used, then repeat the strongest shapes with a different payoff.
5. **Draft OPEN + first line of HOLD + GUESS device + PAY line** for each variant.
6. **Score** against the 7 tests. Kill anything that fails test 7. Rewrite until pass.
7. **Voice pass.** Match the writer's voice profile if they have one: short sentences,
   fragments, no corporate register. First names only. No banned words. Follow the
   platform's pacing rules.
8. **Humanizer pass, then unnatural-phrasing pass** (real reader's ear, then the writer's ear).
9. **Output** in the format below.

## 9. Output format

```
FORMAT: [LinkedIn / email / article / video / ad / LP / outreach]
ICP: [who, and what ground they already stand on]
PAYOFF (written first): [one sentence, literally true]

VARIANT 1 · shape: [gap shape]
[opening lines exactly as they'd ship]
Hold: [what the 2 to 4 lines after it contain]
Guess device: [the invited or pre-empted wrong guess]
Pay line: [the exact sentence that closes it]
Score: Tooth ✓ Goldilocks ✓ Pandora ✓ Shape ✓ Hold ✓ Guess ✓ Payoff ✓

VARIANT 2 ... (as many as the writer asked for, default 5; different shapes)

RECOMMENDED: [which one and why, one line]
```

Variant count = the number the writer asked for (default 5), shapes varied, and always end
with ONE recommendation so the writer can pick. Payoff shown before any hook so the writer
can see the hole is real. If a variant only works with a number the writer has not
confirmed, mark it `[VERIFY: number]` rather than shipping it.

## 10. Quick reference card

- Gap = what they know minus what they just realised they don't. Manufacture the realising.
- Peak curiosity sits where they know enough to feel the hole's shape.
- Ambiguous-with-a-shape beats stated fact (Pandora).
- Wanting isn't liking. Hook borrows wanting. Body owes liking.
- Wrong guess + "wrong" = strongest retention lever there is.
- One gap. Hold with evidence. Pay in the promised shape. Literally true.
- Unpaid gap = clickbait = dampened reader = ten dead hooks after it.
